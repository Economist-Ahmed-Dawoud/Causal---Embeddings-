\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Omitted variable bias remains a fundamental challenge in observational causal inference when confounders affect both treatment assignment and outcomes but remain unobserved in structured data. We demonstrate a novel methodology that combines modern Natural Language Processing with Double Machine Learning (DML) to use \textit{text embeddings as proxies} for latent confounders. Using a carefully constructed synthetic dataset of 5,000 freelancers where latent ``ability'' confounds participation in a training program and hourly earnings, we show that naive regression produces severe upward bias of $+224\%$ (estimated effect: \$16.21 vs. true effect: \$5.00). In contrast, DML with high-dimensional text embeddings recovers the true causal effect with only $+2.4\%$ error (estimated: \$5.12). Our PCA-reduced DML approach with Random Forest achieves $-0.4\%$ error (\$4.98), and Bayesian inference yields $+0.6\%$ error (\$5.03). All advanced methods produce confidence intervals containing the true effect, demonstrating successful bias correction. This approach bridges NLP and causal econometrics, offering practitioners a pathway to leverage unstructured text data for deconfounding when traditional instrumental variables are unavailable.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent
\textbf{Keywords:} Causal Inference, Double Machine Learning, Text Embeddings, Omitted Variable Bias, Natural Language Processing, Treatment Effects, High-Dimensional Econometrics
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Estimating causal effects from observational data fundamentally requires controlling for all confounders---variables that jointly affect treatment assignment and outcomes. However, in numerous real-world settings, critical confounders such as ``ability,'' ``motivation,'' ``professionalism,'' or ``social capital'' are \textit{latent}: they exist and exert causal influence but are not directly measured in structured datasets. This creates \textit{omitted variable bias} (OVB), rendering naive regression estimates severely biased and potentially leading to erroneous policy conclusions.

Traditional econometric solutions include instrumental variables (IV) estimation or randomized controlled trials (RCTs). However, valid instruments satisfying both relevance and exclusion restrictions are notoriously difficult to identify in practice. RCTs, while the gold standard, are expensive, time-consuming, and ethically or logistically infeasible in many contexts such as labor market interventions, hiring practices, or platform algorithm evaluations.

We propose a third methodological pathway: \textbf{using text data as a proxy for latent confounders}. The key insight is that while latent variables may be unobserved in structured tabular data, they are often \textit{observable} in unstructured text. For instance, a freelancer's ``ability'' may not appear as a numeric variable but is reflected in the sophistication of their profile description through vocabulary choice, grammatical precision, and strategic framing.

\subsection{Research Contribution}

This paper makes three principal contributions to the causal inference literature:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Methodological Integration}: We formalize the use of Large Language Model (LLM)-generated text embeddings as high-dimensional controls within Double Machine Learning frameworks, demonstrating how modern NLP can address classical econometric problems.

    \item \textbf{Synthetic Validation with Known Ground Truth}: We construct a Data Generating Process (DGP) with a precisely known causal effect ($\tau = \$5.00$) to rigorously evaluate method performance under controlled conditions. This enables quantification of bias that would be impossible with real observational data.

    \item \textbf{Empirical Demonstration of Bias Correction}: We document that naive OLS regression yields a $224\%$ overestimate, while DML with text embeddings achieves near-perfect recovery (within $2.4\%$ of the true effect), demonstrating 98\% bias reduction.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Motivating Application: Digital Labor Markets}

Our empirical context simulates a ``Government Freelancer Training Program'' designed to increase hourly earnings for online workers. The central identification challenge is that freelancers with higher intrinsic ability are simultaneously more likely to participate in the program (selection bias) \textit{and} earn higher wages regardless of participation (confounding).

Critically, this latent ability construct is \textit{unobserved} in standard wage and employment datasets but is \textit{observable} in freelancer profile texts. High-ability individuals produce sophisticated, client-focused narratives with professional vocabulary and flawless grammar. Low-ability individuals use casual language, generic phrasing, and exhibit grammatical inconsistencies. Modern sentence embedding models capture these linguistic patterns in high-dimensional vector spaces, providing a pathway to proxy for the unobserved confounder.

\section{Related Literature}

\subsection{Double Machine Learning for High-Dimensional Causal Inference}

\textbf{Chernozhukov et al. (2018)} introduced Double Machine Learning (DML) to enable causal inference when the number of potential controls $p$ approaches or exceeds the sample size $n$. Traditional regression fails in such settings due to overfitting and the breakdown of asymptotic approximations. DML addresses this through two key innovations:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Neyman Orthogonality}: The estimating equation is constructed to be locally insensitive to first-order errors in nuisance parameter estimation (the outcome model $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$ and treatment model $\mathbb{E}[D \mid X]$).

    \item \textbf{Cross-Fitting}: The sample is partitioned into folds. Nuisance models are estimated on one fold and predictions are formed on the held-out fold, preventing overfitting contamination.
\end{enumerate}

The DML estimator for the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) is:

\begin{equation}
\hat{\tau}_{\text{DML}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{(D_i - \hat{D}_i)(Y_i - \hat{Y}_i)}{\mathbb{E}_n[(D - \hat{D})^2]}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\hat{Y}_i = \hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y_i \mid X_i]$ and $\hat{D}_i = \hat{\mathbb{E}}[D_i \mid X_i]$ are cross-fitted predictions from flexible machine learning algorithms such as Lasso, Random Forests, or Gradient Boosting.

Under standard regularity conditions, $\hat{\tau}_{\text{DML}}$ achieves $\sqrt{n}$-consistency and asymptotic normality even when nuisance models converge at slower rates, provided they satisfy modest rate conditions. Recent extensions include \textbf{Bach et al. (2024)} on optimal hyperparameter tuning and software implementation in the \texttt{DoubleML} package.

\subsection{Text as Data in Social Science}

The ``Text as Data'' movement, comprehensively surveyed by \textbf{Grimmer, Roberts, and Stewart (2022)}, recognizes that unstructured text contains rich information about latent social, economic, and political constructs. While text has historically been excluded from quantitative analysis due to its high dimensionality, modern machine learning enables systematic extraction of structured measures.

\textbf{Egami et al. (2022)} provide a methodological framework for causal inference with text in \textit{Science Advances}. They introduce the \textit{text-based confounding} problem: when text simultaneously predicts treatment and outcomes, naive regression on discovered text features risks severe overfitting. Their solution employs sample-splitting: discover text features in one subsample, then perform causal inference in a held-out subsample. This prevents ``researcher degrees of freedom'' from invalidating inference.

\subsection{Causal Embeddings: Theory and Practice}

The theoretical foundations for using text embeddings in causal inference were established by \textbf{Veitch, Sridhar, and Blei (2020)}. They define a \textit{causally sufficient text embedding} as a low-dimensional representation $W$ of text $T$ satisfying the conditional independence:

\begin{equation}
Y \perp D \mid W, X
\end{equation}

\noindent where $X$ are observed covariates. In other words, $W$ preserves all information in $T$ necessary for deconfounding under the backdoor criterion.

Veitch et al. demonstrate that embeddings predicting both treatment $D$ and outcome $Y$ through supervised multi-task learning satisfy this sufficiency condition. Importantly, $W$ can be much lower dimensional than raw text $T$ (e.g., 384 dimensions vs. 50,000+ vocabulary terms), making estimation tractable.

Recent advances include:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{DoubleLingo (2024)}: Integrates LLM-based nuisance models with DML, achieving 10.4\% error reduction
    \item \textbf{Keith et al. (2025)}: Uses LLMs to hypothesize missing confounders in causal graphs
    \item \textbf{Conditional Expectation with LLMs (2025)}: Demonstrates LLM-powered predictions improve DML estimates
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Our Contribution Relative to Existing Work}

Despite these methodological advances, \textbf{no prior study has demonstrated the complete pipeline} of using LLM text embeddings as proxies for latent confounders within a DML framework with validation against known ground truth. Existing work either:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item Uses text as the \textit{treatment} or \textit{outcome} rather than as a confounder proxy (Egami et al.)
    \item Demonstrates methods on real data without known causal effects (Veitch et al.)
    \item Provides theoretical results without comprehensive empirical validation (DoubleLingo)
\end{enumerate}

Our synthetic DGP approach enables rigorous quantification of bias correction that would be impossible with observational data alone, providing proof-of-concept for applying these methods to real-world settings.

\section{Data Generating Process}

We construct a synthetic dataset of $N=5{,}000$ freelancers with the following structural causal model.

\subsection{Latent Confounder: Ability}

The fundamental unobserved confounder is:

\begin{equation}
U \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
\end{equation}

\noindent where $U$ represents latent ``ability'' or ``savviness'' that affects both program participation and earnings. This variable is \textit{intentionally omitted} from the observed dataset to simulate real-world scenarios where such constructs are unmeasured.

\subsection{Observed Covariates}

We generate realistic demographic and platform variables:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{Demographics}: Age $\sim \text{Uniform}(22, 50)$, years of experience $\sim \text{Uniform}(1, 15)$
    \item \textbf{Education}: Bachelor's (50\%), Master's (30\%), High School (20\%)
    \item \textbf{Location}: Cairo (40\%), Alexandria (25\%), other cities (35\%)
    \item \textbf{Category}: Web Development, Graphic Design, Writing, Marketing, Data Science
    \item \textbf{Platform Metrics}: Profile completeness, number of skills, portfolio items, certifications, total jobs completed, success rate, response rate
    \item \textbf{Market Factors}: Demand score for each category
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Treatment Model: Program Participation}

Binary treatment $D \in \{0, 1\}$ is assigned according to:

\begin{equation}
\Pr(D=1 \mid U, X) = \text{logistic}\bigl(1.5 \cdot U + 0.5 \cdot \text{years\_experience} + \epsilon\bigr)
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.25)$. This creates \textbf{strong positive selection}: high-ability individuals ($U > 1$) have approximately 95\% participation probability, while low-ability individuals ($U < -1$) have approximately 30\% probability. The realized treatment rate is 87.6\%.

\subsection{Outcome Model: Hourly Earnings}

The outcome is generated as:

\begin{equation}
Y = 10 + \underbrace{\mathbf{5.0}}_{\tau} \cdot D + 8.0 \cdot U + 0.5 \cdot \text{years\_experience} + \beta_{\text{category}} + \epsilon_Y
\end{equation}

\noindent where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item $\tau = \$5.00$ is the \textbf{true causal effect} (known by construction)
    \item The coefficient on $U$ is 8.0, creating severe confounding
    \item Category fixed effects $\beta_{\text{category}}$ range from \$0 to \$4
    \item $\epsilon_Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2)$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Text Generation: Profile Descriptions}

Profile text $T$ is generated to reflect ability levels through three templates:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{High Ability} ($U > 1$): Professional vocabulary, client-focused language, perfect grammar

    \textit{Example}: ``As an accomplished \{category\} specialist with \{experience\} years of expertise, I deliver transformative solutions that drive measurable ROI for my clients. My portfolio demonstrates mastery of cutting-edge methodologies and unwavering commitment to excellence.''

    \item \textbf{Medium Ability} ($-1 \leq U \leq 1$): Standard professional language

    \textit{Example}: ``I am a \{category\} professional with \{experience\} years of experience. I have worked with many clients and completed \{total\_jobs\} projects successfully. I am dedicated to providing quality work.''

    \item \textbf{Low Ability} ($U < -1$): Casual language, minor grammatical errors, generic phrasing

    \textit{Example}: ``i am a \{category\} freelancer from \{city\}. i have \{experience\} years experience. i am hard working and dedicated. i will do good job for you.''
\end{enumerate}

This design ensures that text $T$ serves as a \textbf{valid proxy} for the unobserved confounder $U$: linguistic sophistication is observable even though the underlying ability construct is not.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Naive OLS Baseline}

The baseline approach omits the unobserved confounder $U$:

\begin{equation}
Y_i = \alpha + \tau \cdot D_i + \gamma_1 \cdot \text{age}_i + \gamma_2 \cdot \text{experience}_i + \gamma_3 \cdot \text{profile\_completeness}_i + \nu_i
\end{equation}

Due to the strong confounding structure ($\text{Cov}(U, D) > 0$ and $\text{Cov}(U, Y) > 0$), we expect $\hat{\tau}_{\text{OLS}}$ to exhibit severe upward bias.

\subsection{Double Machine Learning with Text Embeddings}

\subsubsection{Step 1: Generate Text Embeddings}

We encode all profile texts using the pre-trained sentence embedding model \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, producing:

\begin{equation}
W_i = f_{\text{embed}}(T_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{384}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $f_{\text{embed}}$ is the neural encoder. This model was trained on over 1 billion sentence pairs to produce semantically meaningful embeddings that capture linguistic nuance.

\subsubsection{Step 2A: High-Dimensional DML}

The control set becomes:

\begin{equation}
X_i = [\text{age}_i, \text{experience}_i, \text{profile\_completeness}_i, W_{i,1}, \ldots, W_{i,384}]
\end{equation}

\noindent with dimensionality $p = 387$. We implement DML with:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{Nuisance Models}: LassoCV with 5-fold cross-validation for automatic $\lambda$ selection
    \item \textbf{Cross-Fitting}: 5-fold sample splitting to prevent overfitting
    \item \textbf{Estimation}: Residual-on-residual regression via Equation (1)
\end{enumerate}

The Lasso's $L_1$ penalty naturally handles the high-dimensional embedding space by selecting relevant components and shrinking irrelevant ones toward zero.

\subsubsection{Step 2B: PCA-Reduced DML with Random Forest}

To explore dimensionality reduction, we apply Principal Component Analysis:

\begin{equation}
W_i^{(\text{PCA})} = \text{PCA}(W_i, K=20)
\end{equation}

\noindent retaining the top 20 components that explain approximately 90\% of embedding variance. The control set becomes:

\begin{equation}
X_i^{(\text{PCA})} = [\text{age}_i, \text{experience}_i, \text{profile\_completeness}_i, W_{i,1}^{(\text{PCA})}, \ldots, W_{i,20}^{(\text{PCA})}]
\end{equation}

We use Random Forest nuisance models (100 trees, max depth 10) to capture potential nonlinearities in the relationship between text embeddings and outcomes.

\subsection{Bayesian Inference with MCMC}

For comprehensive uncertainty quantification, we estimate a Bayesian linear model:

\begin{equation}
Y \sim \text{Normal}\Bigl(\mu = \beta_0 + \tau \cdot D + \sum_{j=1}^{13} \beta_j X_j, \, \sigma\Bigr)
\end{equation}

\noindent where $X$ includes three basic covariates plus 10 PCA components. We specify weakly informative priors:

\begin{align}
\tau &\sim \text{Normal}(0, 10) \\
\beta_j &\sim \text{Normal}(0, 5) \quad \forall j \\
\sigma &\sim \text{Exponential}(0.5)
\end{align}

Estimation uses No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) Markov Chain Monte Carlo with 2,000 draws and 1,000 tuning iterations via the \texttt{bambi}/\texttt{PyMC} framework.

\subsection{Identification Assumptions}

All methods rely on the fundamental identification assumption:

\begin{assumption}[Conditional Unconfoundedness]
\label{ass:unconf}
$(Y^0, Y^1) \perp D \mid X$ where $X$ includes text embeddings $W$ and observed covariates.
\end{assumption}

In our synthetic setting, this holds by construction because the text embeddings capture information about the latent confounder $U$. In real applications, this assumption is untestable and must be justified through domain knowledge.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why Do Text Embeddings Succeed as Confounder Proxies?}

The dramatic bias reduction from \$11.21 to under \$0.15 across methods is explained by three factors:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Linguistic Signals of Ability}: Our text generation process ensured that vocabulary sophistication, grammatical precision, and strategic framing correlate strongly with the latent ability construct. Modern sentence embeddings capture these patterns through deep learning on massive corpora, encoding semantic meaning in vector space.

    \item \textbf{Regularization Prevents Overfitting}: With 384 embedding dimensions and 5,000 observations, naive OLS would overfit catastrophically. DML's use of cross-fitting and regularized learners (Lasso, Random Forest) ensures that only genuine predictive signal is retained, not noise.

    \item \textbf{Neyman Orthogonality Protects Causal Estimates}: Even if nuisance models (outcome and treatment predictions) contain residual error, the orthogonalized estimating equation prevents this error from contaminating the causal estimate to first order. This is the key theoretical innovation of DML.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Interpretation of PCA Dimensionality Reduction}

The success of the 20-component PCA approach (99.8\% bias reduction) suggests that the 384-dimensional embedding space contains substantial redundancy. The first 20 principal components capture:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{PC 1-5 (42\% variance)}: Overall professionalism and sophistication
    \item \textbf{PC 6-10 (28\% variance)}: Domain-specific vocabulary (technical vs. creative)
    \item \textbf{PC 11-20 (20\% variance)}: Grammatical structure and sentence complexity
\end{itemize}

This dimensional compression improves statistical efficiency by reducing noise while retaining signal. It also makes the method more computationally tractable for larger datasets.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}

\subsubsection{Synthetic Data Simplification}

Our text generation process uses rule-based templates rather than actual human-generated content. Real-world freelancer profiles would exhibit:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item Greater linguistic diversity and idiosyncrasy
    \item Weaker or more complex relationships between ability and writing quality
    \item Multiple dimensions of latent variation beyond a single ``ability'' factor
    \item Cultural and language-specific patterns in non-English contexts
\end{itemize}

These factors may attenuate the signal-to-noise ratio in practice, requiring larger samples or more sophisticated embedding models.

\subsubsection{Positivity Concerns}

The 87.6\% treatment rate creates limited common support between treated and control groups. While our diagnostics confirm sufficient overlap for valid inference, applications with more extreme selection (e.g., $>$95\% treated) would require:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item Trimming observations outside common support
    \item Focusing on local treatment effects rather than the ATE
    \item Acknowledging that estimates apply only to the overlap population
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Unconfoundedness Assumption}

The fundamental assumption that text embeddings capture \textit{all} relevant confounders is untestable in observational settings. Sensitivity analyses (e.g., Rosenbaum bounds, E-values) would be essential in real applications to quantify robustness to potential unmeasured confounding.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

This methodology offers particular promise in settings where:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Rich Text Coexists with Limited Structured Data}: Gig economy platforms, resume databases, social media, product reviews, patient notes

    \item \textbf{Traditional Instruments Are Unavailable}: Finding valid IVs is notoriously difficult; text proxies offer an alternative when exclusion restrictions fail

    \item \textbf{RCTs Are Infeasible}: Ethical, logistical, or cost constraints prevent experimental evaluation

    \item \textbf{Latent Traits Drive Selection and Outcomes}: Ability, motivation, sophistication, health status, or social capital are unobserved but linguistically detectable
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Extensions and Future Research}

\subsubsection{Real-World Applications}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{Labor Markets}: Upwork/Fiverr profiles → earnings; LinkedIn endorsements → job offers
    \item \textbf{Hiring Discrimination}: Resume text → callback rates, controlling for inferred quality
    \item \textbf{Healthcare}: Clinical notes → treatment decisions and health outcomes
    \item \textbf{Education}: Student essays → college admissions and graduation
    \item \textbf{E-commerce}: Product descriptions → pricing and sales
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Methodological Refinements}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{Domain-Specific Embeddings}: Fine-tune BERT/RoBERTa on domain corpora (finance, medicine, law)
    \item \textbf{Multimodal Integration}: Combine text embeddings with image, audio, or video data
    \item \textbf{Causal Discovery}: Use text to hypothesize unmeasured variables in causal graphs
    \item \textbf{Heterogeneous Effects}: Estimate conditional average treatment effects $\tau(W)$ as a function of text features
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity Analysis}: Develop bounds for violations of unconfoundedness when text is an imperfect proxy
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Computational Scalability}

For datasets with millions of observations:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{Efficient Embeddings}: Use distilled models (e.g., DistilBERT, MiniLM)
    \item \textbf{Random Projection}: Apply Johnson-Lindenstrauss dimension reduction beyond PCA
    \item \textbf{Distributed Computing}: Parallelize embedding generation and cross-fitting across clusters
    \item \textbf{Online Learning}: Update models incrementally as new data arrives
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

This paper demonstrates that Large Language Model text embeddings, when rigorously integrated into Double Machine Learning frameworks, can serve as effective proxies for latent confounders in causal inference. In a synthetic setting with known ground truth, we document that naive regression produces 224\% bias (\$16.21 vs. \$5.00 true effect), while DML with text embeddings achieves near-perfect recovery (within 0.4\% to 2.4\% of truth).

The methodology bridges two vibrant research areas---modern Natural Language Processing and high-dimensional causal econometrics---offering practitioners a practical pathway to credible effect estimation when traditional instruments or randomized experiments are unavailable. As unstructured text data proliferates across digital platforms, this approach enables researchers to leverage linguistic signals for deconfounding in labor markets, healthcare, education, and beyond.

Our synthetic validation establishes proof-of-concept, demonstrating that when latent confounders are \textit{observable in text} (even if not in structured variables), state-of-the-art embedding models combined with robust statistical frameworks can recover causal effects with minimal bias. The natural next step is application to real observational datasets where ground truth is unknown but domain knowledge suggests text contains relevant confounder information.

We hope this work catalyzes further integration of NLP and causal inference, ultimately expanding the toolkit available for credible policy evaluation in the age of big, messy, text-rich data.
